Ongoing Projects

Determining the mechanisms of temporal context recovery in autobiographical memory

Lei, D., Barense, M.D., Seger, S., Lohnas, L., & Lega, B.

Episodic recollection depends on recovering not only the content of an event, but also its temporal context. Retrieved-context theories predict that recalling an episode reinstates its encoding context and thereby biases recovery toward temporally adjacent events. However, this key prediction has only been tested in highly controlled, lab-based experiments that lack the rich personal context of autobiographical memory. This project uses HippoCamera to test retrieved-context predictions in real-world autobiographical memory, first behaviourally in healthy adults (data collection nearing completion), and then in epilepsy patients implanted with the NeuroPace RNS system to examine the neural mechanisms. To our knowledge, this is the first study to directly test retrieved-context mechanisms in real-world autobiographical memory.

Investigating the role of sleep in episodic memory consolidation using a naturalistic memory paradigm

Lei, D., Zhang, X., Rafla, D., Zhang, L., & Levine, B.

Over a century of research from the cognitive and clinical neuroscientific literatures has consistently demonstrated an integral role of sleep in episodic memory consolidation and recollection. Although robust, much of the evidence supporting this finding comes from laboratory-based paradigms involving recognition-cued recall for non-naturalistic stimuli such as wordlists. As such, it remains unclear whether sleep benefits the free recall of episodic memories and what neurophysiological mechanisms might be at play in such an effect. Here, we seek to examine the impact of sleep on free recall by using a naturalistic memory design in the form of a controlled hospital tour. Ultimately, we aim to identify the role of sleep by investigating the effect of targeted memory reactivation (TMR) on episodic recall. To our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate the role of sleep for free recall under the context of naturalistic memory.

A digital educational program to improve memory for older adults

Lei, D., Hong, B., Hughes, E. C., & Barense, M. D. (2025) · presentation

Recent data from geriatric research centres around the world show that older adults are keen to use technological tools to improve their well-being, especially in the context of cognitive aging. Among the various tools that have been readily accepted by older adults is HippoCamera — a mobile application designed to enhance memory for everyday events. This smartphone application has drawn great interest from the older adult community with many users suggesting to add some content explaining the science behind the app's effectiveness as a memory tool. Here, we set out to develop an educational program to complement HippoCamera with a specific focus on the different memory strategies established in the memory science literature. In interviews with older adults, we found key factors that drive older adults' engagement with technological healthcare tools. This study highlights the factors that shape older adults' acceptance of technologically-based tools, informing the optimization of future interventions.

A Daoist Perspective of Consciousness

Lei, D. & Djedovic, A. (2025) · publication

This paper formulates a possible embodied account of consciousness by integrating the vantages of ancient Chinese philosophy and medicine and predictive processing theories in cognitive science. It draws on robust empirical evidence from across the cognitive sciences, including contemporaneous work in philosophy of mind, neuropsychiatry, clinical psychology, and cognitive neuroscience to build on an overarching proposal that consciousness is ultimately the product of a synergistic and exaptive relationship between mind and body. I call this formulation the synergistically affectively interoceptively synchronizing mind-body hypothesis (SAIS). This system is characterized by highly adaptive interoceptive active inference that is realized through the dynamic coordination of allostatic affective control and exteroception. Emerging research underscores parallels between the allostatic paradigm of predictive processing and the system of Qi in traditional Chinese medicine and Daoist conceptions of consciousness, supporting the notion that there lies much to be explored within the realm of the mind-body connection. Leveraging these insights, it is highly plausible that the key to understanding consciousness may lie in a revisit of the fundamental processes that govern cognition, allostasis, homeostasis, and autopoiesis.
