About

I am a fourth-year student at the University of Toronto (St. George Campus) pursuing the Psychology Research Specialist Program (Thesis) and a major in Cognitive Science.

An aspiring cognitive neuroscientist, I am interested in deriving an account of the most fundamental elements that constitute consciousness and its crucial properties such as reasoning and problem-solving. Specifically, I am most interested in the following areas: interoceptive rhythms in cognition, creative cognition, and neurotechnology. In particular, I am drawn to how we may use altered states of consciousness (e.g. psychedelics, lucid dreaming) as an explorative apparatus for gleaning insights into the origins of consciousness.

I believe conducting research in these areas will profoundly inform our understanding of how we may best contribute to developing Artificial General Intelligence and all the visceromotor and sensorimotor components that come with consciousness. Developing a cognitive agent will require that we carve consciousness at its joints – an effort that may only be achieved through an interdisciplinary and synoptically integrative investigation of consciousness from the vantages of psychology, linguistics, computer science, engineering, mathematics, anthropology, and the neurosciences.

Beyond research aspirations, I am a diligent individual with a specific skillset in academic writing, data analysis, and teaching. Outside the lab, I train karate, cycle, and practice yoga.

Ongoing Projects

Behavioural & intracranial study

Determining the mechanisms of temporal context recovery in autobiographical memory

Lei, D., Barense, M.D., Seger, S., Lohnas, L., & Lega, B.

Retrieved-context theories predict that recalling a memory reinstates its original context, biasing recall toward temporally nearby events — but this has only been tested in controlled lab settings. This project tests these predictions in real-world autobiographical memory using HippoCamera, first in healthy adults, then in epilepsy patients implanted with the NeuroPace RNS system to probe the underlying neural mechanisms.

Sleep / TMR study

Investigating the role of sleep in episodic memory consolidation using a naturalistic memory paradigm

Lei, D., Zhang, X., Rafla, D., Zhang, L., & Levine, B.

Sleep is known to support memory consolidation, but most evidence comes from lab tasks like wordlist recall rather than naturalistic memories. This project examines how sleep affects free recall of a naturalistic experience — a controlled hospital tour — and uses targeted memory reactivation (TMR) during sleep to probe the underlying mechanisms.

Qualitative Study on Aging and Brain Health

A digital educational program to improve memory for older adults

Lei, D., Hong, B., Hughes, E. C., & Barense, M. D. (2025) · presentation

HippoCamera, a memory-enhancing app for older adults, has driven requests for content explaining the science behind it. This project developed a complementary educational program covering key memory strategies, and interviewed older adults to identify what drives engagement with technology-based memory tools — informing how future interventions can be designed.

Publications

Theoretical paper

A Daoist Perspective of Consciousness

Lei, D. & Djedovic, A. (2025) · publication

This paper proposes an embodied account of consciousness that integrates ancient Chinese philosophy and medicine with predictive processing theories from cognitive science. It introduces the synergistically affectively interoceptively synchronizing mind-body hypothesis (SAIS), drawing parallels between predictive processing and the concept of Qi to suggest consciousness emerges from a dynamic, exaptive relationship between mind and body.

Review article

From ‘Ha Ha!’ to ‘Aha!’: Towards an Integrated Understanding of the Effect of Humour on Insight Problem-Solving

Lei, D. (2026) · publication

Insight problem-solving research has long focused on cognitive flexibility as the key driver of the ‘Aha!’ experience, with little attention paid to humour as a contributing factor. This paper reviews emerging evidence that humour meaningfully facilitates insight, arguing for its inclusion in how we define and study insight problem-solving.
