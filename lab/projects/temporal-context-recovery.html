Background

Episodic recollection depends on recovering not only the content of an event, but also its temporal context. Retrieved-context theories predict that recalling an episode reinstates its encoding context and thereby biases recovery toward temporally adjacent events.

The gap

This key prediction has only been tested in highly controlled, lab-based experiments that lack the rich personal context of autobiographical memory. Whether the same mechanism governs how we recover the events of our own lives remains an open question.

Approach

This project uses HippoCamera to test retrieved-context predictions in real-world autobiographical memory. The first phase tests the behavioural prediction in healthy adults, with data collection nearing completion.

The second phase examines the neural mechanisms in epilepsy patients implanted with the NeuroPace RNS system, allowing intracranial recordings during recall of genuinely autobiographical events.

Significance

To our knowledge, this is the first study to directly test retrieved-context mechanisms in real-world autobiographical memory, bridging a well-established laboratory finding and the memories that structure everyday life.
